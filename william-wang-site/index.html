The Rise of Private Credit

William Wang
October 26, 2026

From niche to $1.5 trillion

US private credit has grown from about $400 billion in 2010 to roughly $1.5 trillion by early 2026, and its next phase will be defined by consolidation, not collapse. Non-bank lenders now make senior, floating-rate loans to middle-market companies that banks once financed.

The 2025–2026 stress episode sharpened the debate. Jamie Dimon’s “cockroach” warning in October 2025 and record redemption requests at non-traded BDCs led some to call private credit “the next 2008.” This paper argues the market is asking the wrong question: the real trends are spread compression, a fragile retail liquidity promise, and capital moving toward the largest platforms.

Who holds the market

Most private credit capital cannot leave quickly: more than 80% is contractually locked, which shapes how stress travels.

VehicleApprox. sizeInvestor liquidity
Closed-end institutional funds$1.0–1.2TLocked for 7–10 years
Non-traded BDCs~$130BQuarterly redemptions, capped and gateable
Listed BDCs~$80BPermanent capital, traded on exchange
Private BDCs~$30BRestricted

The wealth channel is the fastest-growing piece. Non-traded BDCs grew 51% in 2025, against 6% for listed BDCs, and Blackstone’s BCRED alone holds $82 billion. Private credit is no longer only an institutional asset class; it is now tied to household savings.

Trend 1: Stable yields hide a thinner cushion

Headline yields on senior private credit held near 11% through the rate-hiking cycle, but the risk premium inside them shrank by 1.3 percentage points.

Senior first-lien private creditNet yieldRisk-free rateSpread (risk premium)
2012–2022 average9.0%1.4%7.6%
2023–2025 average11.1%4.8%6.3%
Change+2.1 pts+3.4 pts−1.3 pts

Rising SOFR did the work, not credit compensation. Against the 2020 peak, the premium is down nearly 3 points. The 2023–2025 vintages were written in the most competitive lending market on record, so they enter the next downturn with less room for losses. New-issue pricing widened 50–75 basis points in Q1 2026, the first real repricing of the cycle, but spreads remain tight by historical standards.

Trend 2: Retail liquidity meets its first test

Quarterly redemption windows in non-traded BDCs were never true liquidity, and 2025–2026 made that visible. Credit redemptions rose from $1.7 billion in Q2 2024 to $5.7 billion in Q4 2025, a 3.3× increase in 18 months, and several large BDCs received requests above their quarterly caps.

Platforms responded differently. Most gated, as their structures allow. Blue Owl ended quarterly tender offers at OBDC II. Blackstone raised BCRED’s cap from 5% to 7% and added $400 million of firm and employee capital, meeting all $3.7 billion of requests, 7.9% of NAV, without prorating.

Gates are a design feature, not a failure: they stop forced loan sales at distressed prices. Net credit flows stayed positive in Q1 2026, and the next growth lever is policy. US defined-contribution plans hold about $14 trillion, and the August 2025 executive order and the Department of Labor’s March 2026 proposed safe harbor could open 401(k) access.

Trend 3: Stress shows up as lower returns, not a bank run

Private credit is an equity-funded loss-allocation system: losses flow to investors’ returns rather than through deposits and payment systems. Bank exposure is real but contained. US banks had about $95 billion committed to private credit vehicles at Q4 2024, and implied BDC fund-level leverage is roughly 0.56× debt to equity.

A stress test of a BCRED-style fund makes the point. Net return to investors falls from about 9.5% in the base case to 5.5% when losses reach 2.5% of assets, and to about 0.9% in a scenario calibrated to the 2008–2010 crisis. That is return compression, not principal loss, for diversified senior portfolios.

The weak points sit elsewhere:

  • The financing layer: fund-level leverage depends on lenders keeping facilities open, and much of it sits outside supervisory data.
  • Sector concentration: AI-related deals reached 34% of 2025 private credit volume, and many BDCs carry about 20% software exposure.
  • Insurance links: about $900 billion of US insurance liabilities now sit with private-equity-backed insurers.
  • Smaller lenders: concentrated portfolios and thin bank relationships leave less room to refinance. Borrowers under $10 million of EBITDA had covenant default rates above 30% at year-end 2025, against about 1.4% for those above $100 million.

Trend 4: Consolidation around the largest platforms

The most important result of the next credit cycle may be concentration rather than losses. The top five managers already control about 60% of private credit assets, and new entrants are established players such as private equity firms and bank joint ventures, not small boutiques.

Scale matters more in credit than in equity. Upside on a loan is capped while downside can reach full loss, so diversification works by cutting left-tail risk: one 200-loan platform protects investors better than five 30-loan specialists. Large firms such as Apollo, Ares, Blackstone and KKR also combine insurance capital, wealth distribution and multi-bank financing, which lets them absorb stress that would push a smaller lender out. The closer analogy is the hedge fund shakeout after 2000 and 2008, not a banking crisis.

Conclusion

Private credit does not remove credit risk; it changes where losses land and how fast they appear. The defining trends are a thinner spread cushion, a retail liquidity promise now being tested, and capital migrating to scaled, integrated platforms. The question for investors over the next five years is less whether the asset class survives and more which platforms can keep financing, distribution and investor confidence through a downturn.

Communication Essentials for Financial Planners

William Wang
May 2024

Effective communication in financial planning transcends the mere exchange of information; it is about building relationships, understanding client's deepest concerns, and navigating their financial journey with empathy and insight. "Communication Essentials for Financial Planners" by Grable and Goetz serves not just as a guide to mastering these skills but as a manifesto that champions the confluence of technical expertise and human connection.

The book's initial chapters lay a foundation that resonates deeply with the heart of financial planning — understanding the client. The authors' insights into the art of questioning peel back the layers of surface-level financial concerns to reveal the deeply held values and beliefs that drive decision-making. This nuanced approach to communication has proven invaluable, pushing beyond the textbook to the core of clients' financial narratives. It's a lesson in the importance of every story, every detail, and how they cumulatively influence the financial planning process.

Further reflection on the book reveals a powerful alignment with the introspective journey that is integral to my studies. Grable and Goetz don't just instruct on how to listen; they guide planners on how to hear. The distinction is subtle yet profound. Hearing encompasses the words left unsaid, the pauses, the hesitations — the emotional subtext that can often dictate the trajectory of a financial plan. I think this is like making an active listener as you mentioned in class. For example, I could use words that denote moods and states to keep track of my client's moods, such as irrigated, depressed, blamed, paralyzed, and so on. You can then give the customer some targeted responses based on their questions and performance. But the important thing is to really think about the customer, listen to their problems and concerns, and then give them a solution! As I navigated through client interview material and case studies in my coursework, I found myself applying these principles, endeavoring to hear more than what was communicated explicitly.

Moreover, the emphasis Grable and Goetz place on self-awareness catalyzed a transformation in my professional demeanor. Recognizing my biases and values, as the book suggests, has heightened my ability to serve clients with impartiality and genuine understanding. This aligns with my thinking that self-awareness is not a milestone, but an ongoing journey that enriches every interaction with a client.

Balancing qualitative insights with quantitative rigor is another theme that Grable and Goetz approach with finesse. They acknowledge that while numbers tell a story, they don't encapsulate the client's life story. Here, the book echoes the course's advocacy for a balanced approach, where spreadsheets and data are tools, not doctrines. It's a balance that I've learned to navigate, understanding that while data provides the structure of a financial plan, communication styles and techniques breathe life into it.

The most profound impact of "Communication Essentials for Financial Planners" lies in its human-centric approach. I remember one week of discussion where the concept of person- centeredness came through particularly well. We should understand why the client is making these decisions, to think about why he is in so much debt, and there are many clients with complex emotions and deep-seated reasons. We should put people first, listen to them and their stories, understand their problems, and help them better financially. Grable and Goetz explore communication not as a transactional skill but as a relational one. They argue that trust and understanding form the bedrock of effective financial planning. This perspective has not only broadened my view of the planner-client relationship but has also redefined it. It has instilled in me a sense of responsibility that extends beyond fiscal advice to the realm of life coaching.

The discourse by Grable and Goetz on legacy and philanthropy echoes a profound tenet of financial planning—it's not solely about the accumulation of wealth but, more significantly, about the intention behind its utilization. This principle has been a cornerstone of my studies, consistently framing financial objectives not as final destinations but as vehicles to a richer, more purposeful existence. The authors’ insights have honed my skills in guiding client dialogues about the influence of their financial choices and the enduring impact they aspire to have.

Consider the motivations behind a client’s philanthropic actions; it's simplistic to view such gestures as mere tax-saving strategies. Instead, I believe these acts are often driven by a deeper commitment to rectify issues they hold personal or have witnessed, like healthcare deficiencies or educational barriers. They aim to leverage their wealth as a tool for real change, creating value that transcends monetary worth and imbues their existence with true significance.

In summary, 'Communication Essentials for Financial Planners' transcends the traditional confines of a financial planning guide. It acts as a reflective surface for the values, ambitions, and insecurities that clients present at the financial planning table. The book stands as an ode to the eloquence of words, the resonating power of silence, and the profound effect of empathetic listening. It has equipped me to be an attentive listener to my clients' ambitions and needs, to meticulously address their concerns, and to nurture lasting bonds—these are the principles I am committed to upholding in my practice. As I envisage my future role as a financial planner, I recognize that clear, personal communication will be a cornerstone of my profession. We must not only listen to but also truly understand our clients' objectives and worries, respond to them promptly, and establish deep-rooted connections. This text has been instrumental in deepening my understanding of these concepts. Furthermore, it has reinforced my belief in the value of direct, face-to-face interactions with clients, as supported by classical communication theories which assert that the majority of successful communication is achieved through personal, in-person exchanges."

Maximizing Efficiency in Finding a Compatible Partner

William Wang
University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
Department of Finance
December 18, 2024

Introduction

In today's resource-rich environment, individuals face a wide variety of choices and opportunities when selecting a life partner. An economic framework can provide valuable insights into how to efficiently identify a compatible mate. This research explores how key economic factors—such as income, health, and education—impact and optimize the process of finding a suitable long-term partner, with the goal of maximizing benefits and minimizing costs in the decision-making process.

The study will use established economic theories related to marriage, search costs, and matching model, examining how individuals can apply these principles to enhance their success in the modern dating market. Specifically, it will review how individuals can strategically invest in education, health, and income to improve their chances of finding a high-quality partner in an economically efficient way.

Literature Review

The decision-making process of partner selection is influenced by various factors such as education, health, and income, each playing different role in shaping preferences and outcomes. This section examines key findings from existing research to provide a comprehensive understanding of how these economic and personal attributes impact relationship dynamics and mate selection strategies.

Education and Partner Selection

Chade and Lindenlaub (2024) developed a sophisticated matching model to explore how risk-sharing mechanisms influence sorting patterns in partnerships, particularly within marriage markets. Their research reveals that risk-sharing considerations generally promote negative sorting based on skills, where individuals with complementary but differing skill sets pair together to mitigate risks. However, empirical observations in marriage markets often show positive skill sorting, where partners have similar skill levels. Chade and Lindenlaub argue that for positive skill sorting to prevail, the benefits derived from skill complementarities in match output must significantly outweigh the inherent risk-sharing motives. This study provides a novel framework for understanding the conditions under which positive sorting occurs, emphasizing the necessity of substantial skill complementarities to counterbalance the natural inclination towards risk mitigation in partner selection.

Their findings suggest that education can serve as a signal for achieving relationship stability. Positive skill sequencing reflects not only compatibility at the intellectual level but also shared values and lifestyle choices. Individuals who invest in education not only improve their socio-economic status, but also enhance their potential for lasting partnerships based on mutual understanding and shared goals.

Health and Partner Finding

Studies have consistently shown that individuals tend to choose partners with similar health characteristics, a phenomenon known as assortative mating (Otten & Mandemakers, 2023). This similarity extends to various health-related factors: Health behaviors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity levels, Body mass index (BMI), drinking, obesity status, overall health status and health outcomes. In addition, there is strong positive assortative mating in smoking, with smokers more likely to partner with smokers and non-smokers with non-smokers (Banks et al., 2013).

Healthier individuals often exhibit greater self-confidence and social mobility, which may enhance their attractiveness in the dating market. Conversely, relationships themselves can promote healthier behaviors through mutual encouragement and shared routines, such as engaging in exercise or maintaining a balanced diet.

In reverse, disparities in health between partners can lead to friction and strain within relationships. Partners who do not align on health behaviors may face challenges in daily routines or long-term planning, such as child-rearing or retirement. This underscores the importance of health compatibility as a foundational element in relationship stability, suggesting that investments in personal health can improve both individual well-being and romantic outcomes

Income and Relationship

Hawkins, Blanchard, Baldwin, and Fawcett (2015) conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate couple, and relationship education (CRE) programs tailored to low-income couples. Their experimental data suggest that CRE interventions have small but positive effects on relationship quality, communication, and aggression across a diverse low-income population. Stronger positive results were observed in studies involving higher proportions of married couples, “near-poor” participants, and individuals experiencing relationship distress. The authors advocate for innovative curriculum design and enhanced program elements to maximize the impact of CRE programs. This study emphasizes the potential of targeted relationship education programs to improve relationship dynamics and stability for economically disadvantaged couples.

Other studies reveal that income plays a significant role in partner selection. SJ (2013) ’s research highlights a strong tendency for individuals to find partners within their own level of education and income. Moreover, women, in particular, demonstrate a preference for partners with financial stability and upward mobility. Interestingly, as women’s socioeconomic status increases, their emphasis on the economic resources of a potential partner also intensifies. These findings underline the complex interplay of income dynamics and gender-specific mate preferences.

Income inequality further shapes relationship dynamics across socioeconomic levels. Higher socioeconomic status (SES) individuals often prioritize education and career development before committing to long-term partnerships. This deliberate approach not only enhances their economic stability but also allows them to attract partners with similar values and ambitions, creating mutually beneficial relationships. In contrast, lower SES individuals may exhibit greater mistrust of long-term relationships and higher acceptance of non-traditional family structures, such as childbearing outside of marriage. This divergence illustrates how economic factors influence not only individual preferences but also broader social norms surrounding partnership and family life.

In other aspects, Income management also varies significantly by relationship status and cultural background. Cohabiting couples are less likely to pool income than married couples, though cohabiters with marriage intentions are more inclined to share finances. Married couples often continue the money management habits formed during cohabitation. Cross-nationally, in regions where cohabitation is less common, the financial management differences between cohabiting and married couples are more pronounced.

Final insights

When selecting a mate, education, health, and income interact in a complicated and multifaceted way that reflects both systemic and individual factors. This review offers a sophisticated knowledge of how people deal with the difficulties of contemporary relationships by examining these aspects via sociological and economic lenses. In order to effectively capture causality and dynamic changes over time, future research should benefit from combining experimental designs and longitudinal investigations. Such efforts would not only deepen academic insights but also inform practical strategies for individuals and policymakers aiming to foster stable, fulfilling partnerships.

Causation or Correlation: Benefits of Getting Married

It has long been recognized that marriage brings many benefits, including better health, greater financial security, and greater life satisfaction. However, it is unclear whether these advantages are caused by marriage itself or merely reflect pre-existing advantages inherent in deciding to marry the researchers conducted their analysis with controlled trials a longitudinal study was used to distinguish correlation from causation to examine the underlying relationship. For example, Waite and Gallagher (2000) have shown that marriage can improve health by promoting positive behaviors, sharing resources, and providing emotional support. In addition, married individuals tend to adopt healthier eating and exercise habits and engage in more harmful activities than single individuals (Umberson & Karas Montez, 2010).

On the other hand, critics argue that healthier, wealthier, and more connected individuals are just more likely to marry in the first place, thus skewing the data Killewald's (2016) study establishes how life of premarital financial advantage tends to predict marital success and stability, not Conversely. This highlights the importance of accounting for selection bias in analyzing the impact of marriage. Future research could use randomized controlled trials or natural experiments—such as comparisons of outcomes across sites of different mating stimuli—to assess the causal mechanisms involved about it well.

The occupational dimension of marriage benefits also warrants attention. Stable, high- income jobs are often linked to higher marriage rates and longer-lasting relationships. This could be because economic stability reduces stress and provides a conducive environment for relationship growth. Conversely, individuals in precarious employment may struggle with relationship strain, contributing to lower marriage rates and higher divorce probabilities. For example, research by Schneider (2011) found that income instability significantly increases the likelihood of marital dissolution, particularly in households where traditional gender norms place a heavy emphasis on male breadwinning. This suggests that the economic benefits of marriage are deeply intertwined with occupational stability and income levels, further complicating the causation-versus-correlation debate.

Marriage also brings wider social benefits. Married couples are more likely to contribute to community cohesion and economic productivity. Studies such as Wilcox and Du (2010) show that stable marriages lead to better outcomes for children, including higher levels of education and lower crime rates. These findings demonstrate the benefits of fostering stable marital relationships for society. However, clarifying whether these outcomes result from marriage itself or the characteristics of married people remains a key challenge for policymakers and researchers.

Matching Models and Limitations

Matching models, grounded in economic theory, provide valuable frameworks for understanding how individuals select partners based on traits like education, income, and shared values. Becker’s (1973) theory of marriage markets posits that individuals seek partners who maximize their utility, often by pooling complementary resources. For instance, a partner with strong financial stability may pair with one who excels in household management, creating a mutually beneficial relationship. These models have been instrumental in explaining phenomena such as positive assortative mating—where individuals with similar characteristics, like educational attainment, tend to pair together (Schwartz, 2013).

However, matching models are not without their limitations. One significant drawback is their reliance on simplifying assumptions, such as perfectly rational decision-making and complete information. In reality, human relationships are far more complex. Psychological factors, social norms, and cultural influences often play equally important roles in partner selection. For example, emotional compatibility and shared life goals are difficult to quantify but are critical components of successful relationships. Studies by Fisman et al. (2006) illustrate that preferences often deviate from purely economic considerations; for instance, individuals may prioritize physical attractiveness or shared hobbies over income potential.

Moreover, matching models often fail to account for dynamic changes over time. Preferences and priorities can shift based on life circumstances, such as career changes, health issues, or evolving societal norms. Longitudinal studies, like those by Sweeney and Cancian (2004), reveal that economic and educational homogamy has increased over recent decades, reflecting broader societal trends toward greater gender equality and dual-income households. These findings suggest that static models may be insufficient to capture the evolving nature of partner selection.

Another limitation of matching models is their inability to establish causation. While they can demonstrate correlations between traits and relationship outcomes, they cannot definitively prove that certain traits directly lead to successful partnerships. For example, does higher education directly enhance marital stability, or does it simply correlate with other stabilizing factors, such as higher income or better conflict-resolution skills? To address this, researchers have increasingly turned to experimental designs and natural experiments. Examining how relationship dynamics are affected by outside shocks (such as abrupt changes in income or work status) is one strategy. The causal significance of financial stability in preserving partnerships is shown by the findings of studies like Charles and Stephens (2004), which demonstrate that negative income shocks considerably increase the chance of divorce.

Cultural context further complicates the applicability of matching models. For instance, in collectivist societies, family influence and traditional norms often outweigh individual preferences in partner selection (Ghimire et al., 2006). This contrasts with more individualistic cultures, where personal autonomy and romantic love are prioritized. Such variations challenge the universality of matching models and underscore the need for culturally sensitive frameworks.

Lastly, surveys and observational studies frequently provide the data utilized in matching models, and the outcomes of these research may also be skewed by factors including selective sampling bias and self-reported mistake. To appear more popular, people might, for instance, inflate their income or level of education, which would skew the model's projections. Advances in data collection, such as the use of real-time behavioral data from dating apps, could help address these limitations.

Furthermore, matching models can also help with the complexities of modern relationships, particularly in the context of online dating platforms. These platforms offer access to data on personal preferences, interactions, and outcomes, enabling researchers to enhance traditional models with real-time behavioral insights. For instance, algorithms on platforms like Tinder and Bumble optimize user preferences by analyzing swipe patterns, response rates, and profile attributes, effectively functioning as dynamic matching models. Hitsch et al.'s (2010) study suggests that online platforms often reinforce positive assortative mating, as individuals are more likely to connect with partners who share similar educational and socio-economic characteristics. However, these platforms also introduce new dynamics, such as algorithmic bias, which may limit the diversity of potential matches, and the paradox of choice, where an abundance of options can lead to decision fatigue and decreased satisfaction. By incorporating these insights, matching models can more effectively account for the influence of technology on relationship formation, offering a more nuanced understanding of contemporary partner selection processes. Furthermore, leveraging machine learning to analyze large datasets from online platforms holds promise for identifying strategies to help individuals find compatible partners, such as through shared hobbies, educational backgrounds, age ranges, or physical appearances, ultimately bridging the gap between theoretical predictions and real-world behaviors.

In a nutshell, while matching models offer valuable insights into partner selection, their limitations highlight the need for more nuanced and comprehensive approaches. Integrating psychological, cultural, and temporal dimensions into these models could enhance their explanatory power. Moreover, leveraging innovative data sources and experimental methodologies can help address lingering questions about causation versus correlation, ultimately providing a more holistic understanding of how individuals navigate the complex landscape of modern relationships.

Conclusion

This research underscores the diverse nature of partner selection, showing the significant roles, that education, health, and income play in influencing romantic outcomes. By applying economic principles such as utility maximization and cost-benefit analysis, individuals can tell the complexities of the modern dating market more effectively.

For individuals, strategic investments in personal education, health, and financial stability can significantly enhance their competitive advantage in the partnership market. Prioritizing higher education not only improves career prospects but also signals compatibility and shared values to potential partners. Similarly, improving physical health and a healthy lifestyle can also increase self-esteem, leading to a greater desire to be committed partners.

For policymakers, promoting equitable distribution of resources is critical to improving partnership outcomes at the societal level. For example, expanding access to affordable education and health care can level the playing field and enable people from diverse backgrounds to participate in the dating market in a more equal manner. In addition, targeted relationship education programs (e.g., offering some psychology classes) can help reduce relationship instability and promote healthier interactions. The Government can also encourage workplace policies that promote work-life balance, as career-related stress often affects the stability and quality of romantic relationships.

From a societal perspective, reducing income gaps can lead to more stable and equally beneficial partnerships. Income disparities often create barriers in forming a new relationship, particularly for low-income groups. Policies aimed at narrowing the income gap, such as tax benefits and job training programs, can contribute to stronger partnerships.

Future research should continue exploring the dynamic interaction of these factors, integrating experimental methodologies and longitudinal studies to better capture the causal relationships and temporal changes in partner selection dynamics. Further exploration into the influence of emerging trends, such as online dating platforms and shifting cultural norms, could provide valuable insights into modern relationship strategies.

Overall, the key to a more productive search for a compatible partner lies in two essential areas. For individuals, it requires conscious self-investment and informed decision-making, ensuring they are well-prepared for meaningful and stable relationships. For policymakers, the focus should be on fostering an environment that promotes equal opportunities in education, health, and income growth, thereby creating a society where fulfilling and enduring partnerships can thrive.
